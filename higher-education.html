Case study — Higher Education

Interactive Higher Education Toolkit (SCORM)

Client: University of Westminster · Education for Sustainable Development (SDG 4.2)
Articulate Storyline SCORM ESD / SDG 4.2 Academic staff CPD Interactive toolkit

This project was designed to support a large higher education institution in embedding Education for Sustainable Development (ESD), aligned to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 4.2, across teaching and learning practice. The ambition was not just to raise awareness, but to create a practical, scalable solution that enabled academics to confidently apply ESD principles within their own disciplines.

At the core of the solution was an interactive, SCORM-compliant toolkit built in Articulate Storyline. This provided a structured yet flexible learning experience, allowing academic staff to engage with the content in a way that suited their context, time constraints, and level of prior knowledge. The experience followed a clear journey — introducing key concepts, exploring real-world applications, and then supporting translation into practice.

To bring the content to life, the toolkit integrated a rich blend of media and resources. This included a series of short, focused videos featuring expert perspectives and authentic case studies, helping to ground theory in real teaching environments. Alongside this, downloadable PDFs offered deeper dives into specific themes, providing practical guidance, frameworks, and prompts that academics could take away and use in their own modules and course design.

The course structure itself was intentionally designed around application. Rather than presenting ESD as a standalone concept, the content was framed through familiar academic challenges — such as curriculum design, assessment, and student engagement. This ensured the learning felt immediately relevant and reduced the barrier to adoption. Each section built towards a toolkit of adaptable resources, enabling academics to move from understanding to action with confidence.

A key strength of the project was its focus on usability and integration. The toolkit was designed to fit seamlessly into existing academic workflows, recognising the time pressures and competing priorities faced by staff. Clear navigation, concise content, and practical outputs ensured that engagement translated into meaningful change, rather than remaining as passive learning.

Since implementation, the toolkit has been used across the organisation as a central resource for embedding ESD and supporting the institution’s broader sustainability agenda. It has enabled a consistent yet flexible approach, allowing different faculties and disciplines to interpret and apply SDG 4.2 in ways that are authentic to their subject areas, while still aligning to a shared institutional vision.

Ultimately, the project demonstrates how thoughtfully designed digital learning can move beyond awareness-raising to drive real change in practice. By combining expert insight, applied learning, and accessible resources within a single, cohesive experience, the toolkit supports academics to embed sustainability meaningfully — not as an add-on, but as an integral part of teaching and learning.

Academic Professional Development — Community-Engaged Learning Toolkit (SCORM)

Client: University of Westminster in partnership with the NCCPE (National Co-ordinating Centre for Public Engagement)
Articulate Storyline SCORM / microsite NCCPE partnership University of Westminster Academic staff CPD

This project was created to address a clear need across the higher education sector: while community-engaged learning was gaining momentum as a powerful pedagogical approach, there was no central, accessible resource that brought together good practice in a way academics could actually use. Colleagues were often developing projects in isolation, learning through trial and error, and missing opportunities to draw on the rich experience already present across institutions. The ambition was to change that, and to do so in a way that felt practical, generous, and rooted in the realities of academic life.

Developed in partnership with the National Co-ordinating Centre for Public Engagement (NCCPE) and the University of Westminster, the toolkit was designed as an open-access, SCORM-compliant experience hosted on a dedicated microsite. This ensured it could be freely accessed by colleagues across the sector, while still offering the structure and interactivity of a considered learning journey. The design placed usability and discovery at its heart, giving academics the freedom to explore in a way that matched their own interests and needs.

At the core of the experience is an exploratory model built around a central conference floor. From here, learners navigate six distinct areas, each focused on a key dimension of community-engaged learning, from initial project set-up through to delivery, partnership, and reflection. This spatial metaphor gives the toolkit a sense of place and progression, mirroring the feel of attending a live event while allowing users to dip in and out on their own terms.

To ground the content in real-world practice, the toolkit features ten 30-minute interviews with leading voices from across the sector. These conversations capture authentic insight, lived experience, and hard-earned lessons, offering academics a direct line to colleagues who have built and delivered community-engaged projects themselves. Accompanying each of the six sections is a dedicated handout PDF, distilling the key themes into practical guidance that academics can take away and apply when designing their own courses and modules.

The toolkit was intentionally framed as academic professional development, positioning community-engaged learning not as an optional extra, but as a meaningful part of course design and pedagogy. By focusing on the practical decisions academics face, such as how to build partnerships, structure assessment, and support students through community-based work, the experience helps translate principle into practice. It respects the time and expertise of its audience, offering depth without demanding a significant upfront commitment.

Since launch, the toolkit has been presented at the national NCCPE conference and accessed by over 50 colleagues from across the sector, with continued uptake as part of the open-access offer. Its reach reflects the appetite that exists for shared, high-quality resources in this space, and the value of bringing good practice together in one accessible place.

Ultimately, the project shows how thoughtful digital design can strengthen a whole community of practice. By combining expert voices, structured exploration, and practical outputs, the toolkit helps academics move from curiosity to confident delivery, and supports community-engaged learning to thrive across the sector.

Case study — Higher Education

Authentic Learning Films

Client: University of Westminster · academic storytelling series
Film production Vimeo playlist Academic storytelling Student voice University of Westminster

This project was developed for the University of Westminster to spotlight and share the rich variety of authentic learning happening across its modules and programmes. The ambition was to move beyond written case studies and create something with more immediacy and emotional pull, a resource that could bring good practice to life in the voices of the people delivering and experiencing it. The result is a growing library of storytelling films designed to inspire, inform, and spark new ideas across the academic community.

At the heart of the project is a collection of over 20 short films, each running between three and five minutes and focused on a specific example of authentic learning in action. Every film is built around a case study narrative, weaving together interviews with academic staff and the voices of students who have taken part in the module. This combination is what gives the series its particular power. Hearing directly from students about how a piece of learning landed, what shifted for them, and why it mattered provides a level of insight that no written summary can replicate.

The films were produced with a clear storytelling craft, designed to feel human and grounded rather than polished to the point of feeling corporate. Each one captures the distinct character of the module and the people behind it, allowing different disciplines, pedagogies, and creative approaches to come through in their own voice. Across the series, this creates a vivid picture of what authentic learning looks like in practice, from experiential projects and live briefs through to community partnerships and industry collaboration.

Rather than sitting within a single module or programme, the films were designed as standalone artefacts, freely available and easy to share. They are gathered together in a curated playlist on Vimeo, giving academic colleagues a single destination where they can browse, explore, and draw inspiration from the breadth of practice on show. This approach removes barriers to access and allows the films to travel widely, whether dropped into a team meeting, shared in a professional development session, or picked up by colleagues working on their own module design.

The primary audience for the series is academic staff, and the intent is firmly rooted in professional development and peer-to-peer learning. By showcasing colleagues sharing their own practice openly, the films help to build a culture in which good ideas circulate freely and academics feel encouraged to experiment, adapt, and develop their own approaches. They also serve as a quiet celebration of the craft and creativity that often goes unseen within day-to-day teaching.

Since launch, the collection has been viewed over 5,000 times and has become a well-used resource across the university, helping to promote new ideas and spark conversations about teaching and learning at every level. Its reach reflects both the appetite for this kind of storytelling and the quality of the practice on display.

Ultimately, the project demonstrates how thoughtful, well-crafted film can become a powerful engine for academic development. By putting real voices and real stories at the centre, it turns good practice into something colleagues can see, feel, and take forward into their own work.
