Reboarding: The Onboarding Nobody Builds

Chris Peach · 2 November 2026 · Onboarding & reboarding6 min read

There's a snail in a Julia Donaldson book who spends her days on a rock, itching to see the world. Then a whale swims by, and she hitches a ride on his tail, and sees everything. Icebergs. Volcanoes. A sea that never ends. Most people remember the adventure. I remember the feeling of the ending, the smallness of it. She comes home to the same rock and the same friends, and she is the one who has changed. She's been somewhere, and she's carrying all of it, and it doesn't quite fit anywhere yet.

The book makes it look easy. She's home, and that's that. But finding your place on the rock again isn't something that just happens. Left alone, she's a small snail on a very familiar rock, with a lot to say and nobody who's made room for it.

It's a story that makes me think about what it's like to come back into work. Someone walks back into an organisation they've worked at for years, after a long time away. They know where the fire exits are. They know the people, or most of them. But they've been somewhere, doing something, whatever that was, and the place they've come back to has moved on too. The laptop works, eventually. The inbox has hundreds of emails in it, and everyone assumes they'll pick it up, because they've been here for years.

This might sound familiar, and not because it's one organisation's story. It happens everywhere. What we call onboarding generally stops at the front door, and it's one of the biggest gaps e-learning can support people with.

Nobody builds the onboarding for someone who already works here.

What is reboarding?

Reboarding is onboarding for people who are already part of the organisation: someone coming back after parental leave, a long absence, a sabbatical, a secondment or a career break. I've seen it called a few different things, and in practice most organisations don't call it anything. The person has needs just like any new starter, and it's the same question we ask: what does this person need, and when, to feel confident, capable and connected again?

Induction thinking has nothing to offer a returner. If induction is the presentation, the mandatory training and the fire exits, then a returner is a person who's already done it. So nothing gets built for them. But what they need isn't less than a new starter needs, it's different. They need to catch up on what changed, and they need to feel they still belong.

That's where it goes wrong, and the research points the same way. The consultancy & Culture, reported in June 2025, found that only 17% of working mothers felt confident about returning after maternity leave, and more than half were unhappy with their employer's support. It isn't only about parental leave either. DWP research from 2023 into returning after long-term sickness found most participants had no formal return plan. That was a small qualitative study, so I wouldn't quote it as a statistic, but it fits the pattern. These studies don't prove that a missing pathway caused those experiences, but they do show that coming back is far from smooth.

I'm not going to give examples of every situation, because we know them. The point is what they have in common. Someone has been away, things have moved on, and an organisation assumed it would sort itself out.

Things change, and that's where the interventions come in

While someone is away, the tools change, the processes change, the people change and sometimes the strategy changes. The returner doesn't know any of that, and everyone around them assumes they do. That's the invisible gap, and it's where SOPs and refreshers earn their place. Two things need to happen: the information has to land, and the belonging has to be rebuilt. I'd always do that with a mix of interventions, not one:

  • Bespoke e-learning on what's changed. A short set of modules covering the systems they'll use, the processes that have moved on and the compliance they've missed, sorted before they walk back in, not in week one while they're also trying to find their feet. It's the part of reboarding that e-learning does best, because it's the same facts for everyone and it can sit there waiting for them at their own pace.
  • Video that's easy to keep current. The reason content like this goes stale is that nobody wants to re-film it every time something changes. Avatar video, with a tool like Synthesia, means an update can be recorded once and redeployed quickly, without re-filming anyone, so the refresher stays accurate.
  • A personalised AI coach. Returners don't all need the same thing. An AI coach, working from approved, up-to-date guidance, can help each person find what's relevant to their own role, answer the "where do I find..." questions without anyone feeling daft for asking, and point them to the right refresher at the right moment. It only knows what you give it, so it has to be set up carefully and sit alongside real people, but that's the sort of thing AI is genuinely good for.
  • Mentoring and coaching. A buddy or mentor who's been through the same return, and coaching conversations that help someone get their confidence back, are the bit no module can do. This is where the belonging gets rebuilt, and it's a mainstay of any academy structure, yet it's the part that's easiest to overlook, because it's harder to build into a process.
  • Face-to-face time that's actually worth having. The online part does the heavy lifting on facts so the human part can do what humans are good at: a proper conversation with the team, a welcome back that doesn't feel like a tick-box, a chance to say what they need.

It's e-learning with support and face-to-face training around it, never one on its own.

Reboarding is the side door onto the pathway.

The line manager is the one who needs the confidence

The line manager is usually the person who actually runs the return, and the person nobody has helped. Organisations may have policies and learning for returners, but it's the manager who turns them into a decent first week.

You can't put every manager in a room for a day. But you can give them confidence asynchronously: a short, practical bit of learning they can do when the return is coming up. What's changed in the team since the person left? What should the first week and the first month look like? How often should they check in, and what do they say in that first conversation? It's about doing the right things at the right time, so the manager isn't working it out on the day.

There's always a pathway

There's always a pathway. Reboarding isn't a separate project, it's the route back into one: competence, growth and capability.

Back in the building, but not back in the organisation.

The aim isn't simply to get someone back through the door. It's to help them find their place again, and that doesn't happen on its own, for the snail or for anyone.

If you're not sure what your organisation has for the person coming back, it might be worth finding out before they walk in. Look at what a returner would actually get on their first Monday, and what their manager would be given.
