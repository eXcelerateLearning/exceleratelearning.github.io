Knowledge Is Depth, Understanding Is Breadth

Chris Peach · 3 November 2026 · Learning design6 min read

Most learning only goes one way: down.

You pick a subject, you go deeper, you become an expert. That's vertical learning, and it matters. On its own it produces people who know a huge amount about one thing, and that's fine in some situations.

The other direction is horizontal: learning across disciplines, seeing how your subject connects to the ones next to it, and understanding what other people's expertise looks like from the inside. On its own, that gives you people who know a little about a lot, and that's fine in some situations too. But breadth without depth has nothing solid to stand on, and depth without breadth can't see beyond itself. Each one needs the other.

My view: knowledge is depth. Understanding is breadth. A learner needs both.

Where I first saw it

It was working with Citizens UK on a module for community organising that made me see it. Community organising is collective by nature. It depends on people with different experience, different perspectives and different levels of influence sharing what they know to reach a shared goal. You can't learn that on your own.

That's when I started thinking differently about how we design learning. Not just vertically, taking someone deeper into a subject, but horizontally too, helping them understand the people and perspectives around it. Vertical and horizontal pathways together have more impact for the future and for the world we're in, because people share their experience and their knowledge instead of keeping it in separate lanes.

Why it matters now

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 found that around seven in ten employers see analytical thinking as an essential skill, and about two-thirds rank resilience, flexibility and agility among their core skills. Employers also expect 39% of workers' core skills to change by 2030.

Those aren't just technical skills. They need people to think beyond their immediate responsibilities, recognise other perspectives and respond to problems that rarely sit neatly inside one discipline. Specialist knowledge still matters, but it can't be the whole picture. That goes for any learner, whether they're in a corporate academy or on a degree pathway in HE. We're building critical thinkers for the future, and the 21st century needs critical thinkers who have skills and understanding (broad) and knowledge (depth). Learning that is authentic and transformative needs both, designed in on purpose, not left to chance.

What it looks like in practice: One Health

I've just finished a six-month project. We delivered a 3-hour One Health antimicrobial resistance course as part of a learning pathway for students across Heidelberg University, the University of Milan, Charles University and the University of Warsaw, part of the 4EU+ European University Alliance. Human medicine, animal sciences and environmental sciences: three disciplines that have to work together on a problem none of them can solve alone.

A veterinary student doesn't need to become a clinician or an environmental scientist. But they do need to understand how those professionals approach the same problem. So the design has three layers:

1. Your discipline in depth. Each learner chooses their discipline and gets a section on what their field needs for One Health and interdisciplinary work. They can then access the broader content for their own discipline too, so they get both.

2. Other disciplines, for understanding. They work through the other two perspectives, so a vet student sees what a clinician sees, and an environmental scientist sees what both of them see.

3. Everything brought together. Everything converges on a single E. coli case study. The discipline is the anchor, and the case study is the angle. Learners complete the content and the quizzes and scenario activities for each discipline before the case study unlocks. When a discipline is complete, its block on the central model changes colour and they move to the next. As more of the picture opens up, the connections between disciplines get clearer.

That unlocking is the point. Three separate courses, one per discipline, can't do it. A simple linear course that delivers content can't either, because it never shows how the pieces interact. The structure of the learning is part of the teaching, not just a way of delivering it.

Along the way, four AI avatars guide learners: one for each discipline and one for the whole journey, so each perspective has its own voice.

How we got there

It started in Milan. I was invited along and asked to take an agenda item, and it grew from there. Over two days we mapped the project out with the academic teams: groups of professors and colleagues who are brilliant in their fields but hadn't yet seen how it all fitted together as a learning journey. I've made a career of making the complex simple, and the work was to do that with them: with empathy, with humour, with rigour, and with questions when they were needed.

On the second day I was asked to lead the session on the face-to-face elements and the project as a whole, so everyone could see how the e-learning feeds the in-person work. That trust mattered. We worked forwards across the project, then backwards from the end. If we do X and Y, do we get to Z? And if we want Z, what must have happened before it, Y and then X?

By the time we left, we had consensus. We built some templates, and each subject expert then wrote the content for their own discipline against shared learning outcomes, all pointing at the same case study.

Knowledge outside, discussion inside

Breadth isn't built by giving people more content. It's built by giving them the chance to engage with other perspectives.

That's why I think asynchronous learning can bring cohorts together, not push them apart. The theory and background can arrive with people before they meet. The classroom is then for questioning, challenging and discussing what they've learned alongside people from other fields, and the application happens afterwards. Knowledge outside the room, discussion in it, application back outside.

The evidence for flipped learning is good. A 2021 analysis of 15 meta-analyses, covering more than 156,000 post-secondary learners, found flipped classrooms beat traditional teaching, with a mean effect size of 0.37. That's an average, not a guarantee that every flipped classroom works, and the research doesn't test my distinction between knowledge and understanding. But it supports the principle, and my own view is that the time in the room is best spent building understanding together.

Beyond higher education

I won't call any of this innovative. Interdisciplinary learning has been around in higher education for a long time. What's less common is seeing it deliberately designed into organisational learning pathways, rather than left to individual experience or goodwill, and that's where I think eXcelerate makes a difference.

Take a financial adviser. Most development is about their own role: technical knowledge, regulation, client skills. How deliberately do we help them understand the compliance, paraplanning and client-service perspectives around them? That understanding usually comes by accident, if it comes at all.

Whether the "disciplines" are medicine, veterinary science and environmental science, or the different functions and levels in an organisation, the principle holds.

Knowing more, understanding more

We spend a lot of time designing learning to make people better at their jobs. We should spend just as much helping them understand the jobs, perspectives and expertise around them.

Depth gives people the confidence and capability to contribute. Breadth helps them see where that contribution fits and how it connects with everyone else's. One without the other leaves something missing.

The best learning doesn't just produce people who know more. It produces people who understand more.
