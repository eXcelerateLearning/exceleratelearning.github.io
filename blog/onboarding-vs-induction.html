Onboarding vs Induction: What the Words Actually Mean (and Why the Difference Is Worth Money)

Chris Peach · 27 October 2026 · Onboarding & induction5 min read

I say "induction" too. It's the word anyone in HR or Learning and Development has heard hundreds of times. We put it in the calendar invite, we say it to our colleagues, but for me it's not the right word anymore. The word I reach for when I'm trying to describe what a new starter needs is onboarding. And I don't think it's an accident that the two words have drifted apart. One describes an event. The other describes a journey. And at the centre of that journey is a person trying to find their feet.

Induction is an output. Onboarding is an outcome.

Here's the distinction I use to scope and price real projects, and it isn't really about the dictionary. It's about the thinking behind the process. Induction often becomes the event: the presentation, the mandatory training, the introduction to the organisation. Onboarding starts with a bigger question: what does this person need, and when, to become confident, capable and connected in their role?

You can call that process induction if you like. What matters is whether it ends when the welcome is over, or carries on until the person has a genuine foothold in the organisation.

Anyone who has started a new job knows the feeling. You want to make a good impression. You don't want to ask the question that sounds obvious. You're not sure who to go to. Nobody goes back to the induction slides, but it's the only thing you have that decides whether someone settles in or quietly feels that sense of fear and regret.

That's why, at eXcelerate Learning, we lean our clients toward "onboarding". It's human, it's longer, and it's about embedding. It's the more honest word for what good practice now looks like. The content matters, but it's there to look after the person, not the other way round.

We make it so that people arrive feeling expected.

I'm working with an energy sector client at the moment on exactly this. The brief wasn't "write us an induction," it was closer to the Amazon model: everything a new starter needs, from HR paperwork through to safety training, sorted before day one. Automations, tasks, learning, all of it in place before anyone walks through the door. The aim is that the first day is about meeting people, not chasing paperwork.

Compare that to the default most organisations still reach for: a PowerPoint and a room full of people, a day that can genuinely feel like a write-off for everyone in it. That's not a dig at anyone doing it, it's just what "induction thinking" produces when it's the only tool in the box. The alternative isn't more content, it's a different shape. Push the admin, the compliance and the foundations online, bespoke and built properly, and you free up the thing that's actually worth doing face-to-face: bringing people together, quarterly, say, to meet each other, ask the real questions and feel part of something. Blended, not either/or. The online part does the heavy lifting so the human part can do what only humans are good at. It's the same thinking behind a bespoke induction rather than a generic one.

Scope an induction and you'll get a day. Scope onboarding and you get a pathway.

If you want the clearest example of "induction" thinking running out of road, look at reboarding: people coming back after parental leave, long-term sickness or a sabbatical. Research from the consultancy & Culture, reported in June 2025, looked at working mothers returning after maternity leave. More than half said they were unhappy with the support from their employer, and only 17% felt confident about going back.

That's what happens when an organisation only ever built an induction. A returner already knows where the fire exits are, so nothing gets rebuilt for them, when what they need is someone to help them feel like they belong again, just shaped differently. That's a big enough topic to deserve its own post, so consider this the trailer.

Why onboarding is the part that matters

CIPD research with Omni RMS, published in September 2024, found that 41% of UK employers who had recruited in the past year had experienced new employees resigning within their first 12 weeks. That doesn't mean 41% of new starters left, but it is a telling window. The welcome has happened. They're still learning the job, building relationships and working out whether the organisation matches what they expected. Whatever was going wrong, nothing was still in place to catch it.

Good onboarding is the thing that's still there at week twelve: clear expectations, chances to practise, access to the right people and regular conversations about progress. It's what turns a new starter into someone who's capable, confident and staying.

Onboarding is where an academy starts

This is the bit I find most exciting. Get onboarding right and you've already built the front door of an academy. The pathway doesn't stop at week twelve. It carries on: the next skills, the next role, the people who've been there years and want to grow. An LMS is where content lives. An academy is what a person belongs to, and for most organisations, onboarding is the first moment that belonging is either built or missed. I wrote more about that in why an academy is a route, not a platform.

I still say "induction" without thinking, same as everyone else. But I'm reaching for "onboarding" more deliberately these days, not because it's the trendier word, because it's the accurate one. The word you use first is usually the ceiling on what gets built.

If your induction ends after the first day, it might be worth asking what happens next.
